How I think and work
Influencing without authority · Procedure-building
"If the outcome is the same, let's at least do it the right way."
A manager wanted to promote someone immediately, with no internal advertising or interview process. In an organisation with no formal promotion procedures yet, it would have been easy to let it pass. Instead I reframed it: not as a block, but as an opportunity. I explained that we could still arrive at the same outcome, but that we owed the team a fair process, especially since someone else had been covering the role in the interim.

We opened the role internally, ran a structured interview process, and his candidate was selected. The team saw a process they trusted. The manager got his outcome. And the organisation had its first promotion procedure on record.
What this showsI find ways to bring leaders with me rather than simply saying no. I think about second-order effects so the business doesn't find out the hard way.
Diagnosing before deciding · Multi-jurisdiction ER
"Before I advise on a solution, I need to understand the real problem."
A manager in the Netherlands came to me wanting to put an employee on a PIP, and reading between the lines, was hoping it would lead to an exit. In the Netherlands, employment law makes this path far more complex than in the UK.

Before going anywhere near a PIP, I asked a lot of questions. What specifically wasn't being delivered? What had the manager tried? The more I listened, the clearer it became: the employee was demotivated and the manager was the primary cause. No recognition, no support, no development conversations.

I redirected the conversation entirely. This was a management issue, not a performance one. The PIP was never needed.
What this showsI don't take the presenting problem at face value. I dig for root causes and give leaders honest feedback, even when it's not what they came to hear.
Cultural agility · In-the-moment adaptation
"Knowing the rule matters less than knowing when to adapt gracefully."
When hiring in Sweden, we followed our standard process and selected a candidate. What we hadn't anticipated was a strong cultural expectation: in Sweden, it's common for the wider team to be involved in the hiring decision, not just the manager.

Rather than dismiss it or restart from scratch, I proposed a compromise: a relaxed coffee chat between the candidate and the team. Informal enough to respect the Swedish collaborative culture, structured enough that it didn't derail our timeline. It worked. The team felt heard. The hire went ahead. And we updated our process for future Swedish recruitment.
What this showsAdapting to cultural norms isn't just about knowing them in advance. It's about responding well when you encounter them.
Stakeholder management · Speaking different languages
"The same proposal needs a different conversation depending on who's in the room."
When proposing a benefits package for Northern Europe at Interhome, I had three very different conversations with three different stakeholders. With the Country Manager, it was detailed and collaborative. With the Director of People, it was higher-level with regional context. With the CFO, I led with engagement survey results then structured the proposal in three tiers: tax-free benefits, easy wins like volunteer days, and phased external vendor options to support budget planning.

He chose not to proceed with external vendors. I accepted that. I'd given him everything to make an informed decision and secured what was achievable without burning goodwill.
What this showsI read the room and adapt, not just my tone, but what I emphasise, what I leave out, and what I ask for. Different stakeholders need different things from the same conversation.
I'm direct, warm, and genuinely invested in the leaders I support. I ask questions before I give answers, and I'll tell you what I think, even when it's not what you were hoping to hear.

How I enter new contexts
Learning the business first
Tour before you build
When I joined Interhome, a property management business very different from the hotel groups I'd come from, I started with a listening tour across all four countries. I met people at every level to understand what they actually did before I touched a single policy. That groundwork led directly to TOIL and time-recording frameworks that were both EU-compliant and operationally grounded.
Scale & pace adaptation
From 800 to 40 and thriving
Moving from The Ned (800 employees, large HR team) to Interhome (40 employees, standalone HR) was a significant shift in pace, structure, and autonomy. I adapted quickly, learned to plan around the organisation's rhythm rather than fight it, and held the full people agenda without a team around me.
Winning over sceptics
The 20-year veteran who became an advocate
At Interhome, the most resistant voice was a Finance accountant who had been there over 20 years. Rather than work around him, I brought him in. I showed him how Rippling would reduce his own manual workload and trained him personally. He became one of the strongest advocates. When team initiatives had to pause for a month, he was the one asking when they'd be back.
Cross-cultural working
Different countries, different norms
Across the UK, Belgium, Netherlands and Sweden, I've learned that the same HR process can't be applied uniformly. Dutch employment law demands a fundamentally different approach to performance management. Swedish culture expects collective involvement in decisions. I navigate these differences by asking the right questions and building local knowledge quickly.
On the industry question
I've worked in luxury hotels in New York, a corporate hotel group across the Americas, a members' club in London, a student union, and a European travel-tech company. What transferred every time wasn't industry knowledge. It was the ability to understand a business quickly, build trust with its leaders, and design people solutions that fit how the business actually works. That's the skill. The industry is the context.

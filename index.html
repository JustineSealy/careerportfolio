How I actually think and work
Influencing · Process-building · Fairness
"If it's still the same person at the end, great — but let's do this properly."
A manager came to me wanting to promote someone immediately — no process, no internal advertisement. The business had no formal promotion procedure at all. Rather than simply blocking him, I reframed the conversation: if we open this up, run a fair process, and the same person wins — you get exactly what you wanted, and you've also set a precedent that protects you next time.

We built a simple internal process from scratch, opened the role to the team, and ran interviews. The manager's preferred candidate was appointed. But the team felt heard, a second employee who'd been covering the role informally wasn't left feeling overlooked, and the business had its first proper promotion framework in place.
What this shows: I don't just say no to leaders — I find the path that gets them what they need while building something that lasts. And I think about the ripple effects on the wider team, not just the immediate decision.
Curiosity · ER judgement · Manager development
"Before we put anyone on a PIP, help me understand what's actually going on."
A manager in the Netherlands came to me wanting to put an employee on a performance improvement plan — with a view to exit. In the Netherlands, the legal bar for this is high, so before going anywhere near a PIP, I asked him to walk me through everything: what had changed, what he'd observed, what conversations had taken place.

As I asked questions, a different picture emerged. The employee wasn't underperforming — they were disengaged. And when I dug further, it became clear the manager hadn't been providing recognition, feedback, or any meaningful support. The issue wasn't capability. It was management.

I redirected the conversation. We talked about what the manager could do differently: regular check-ins, acknowledgment of contributions, clearer expectations. The performance concern resolved without a PIP ever being raised — and the manager came away with a better understanding of their own role in the situation.
What this shows: I don't accept the initial framing of a problem. I ask questions until I understand the real situation — and I'm willing to tell a manager, respectfully, when the issue sits with them. That protects the business legally and builds better managers over time.
